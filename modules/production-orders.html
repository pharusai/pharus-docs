Production Orders

A production order subcontracts the full manufacture of a product. You place the order with a manufacturer, Pharus explodes the product's bill of materials down to raw materials, and the finished goods come back fully costed: the per-unit production cost plus the cost of the materials consumed.

Work order or production order?

Both turn inputs into finished goods through a subcontractor. The difference is the cost model. A work order prices only the conversion (you own the materials, the site provides labour). A production order prices the whole thing, the manufacturer's production cost plus the consumed material cost, so the finished good lands fully costed.

What it does​

  • Track the job. At its core a production order is a record of an outsourced manufacture: which products you expect back, the materials they consume (exploded from the bill of materials), and what has been produced so far. That record stands on its own, with editable dates and no inventory required.
  • Move inventory both ways, fully costed. It demands and allocates the materials as the order commits and goes into production, then on receipt consumes those materials at their weighted-average cost and produces the finished goods at material cost plus production cost. It writes to both the materials and finished-goods ledgers.

How it works​

A production order is always in exactly one status. What changes that status falls into three buckets: some you control by hand, some happen automatically as production is received, and two are endpoints. The sections below take them in that order, then cover what it all does to inventory and to the dates.

The statuses​

You set these by hand. They carry no receipts yet, so you can move between them freely, in either direction, to fix a mistake.

  • Draft. You are still preparing the order. Nothing is committed and inventory is untouched.
  • Entered. The order is real. The output products are booked as on order and their materials are demanded.
  • Placed. Placed with the manufacturer.
  • Production. Manufacture is underway, so the materials are allocated.
  • In Transit. The finished output has been produced and a pickup is scheduled or it is on the road, but it is not yet received into inventory.

Receipts drive these. You never select them; recording a delivery of finished product is what puts the order here.

  • Partial. Some of the output has been produced, but not all.
  • Received. Everything ordered has been produced.

Endpoints.

  • Completed. The order is closed. It can be closed from Received, or closed short from Partial when you decide the partial run is final.
  • Cancelled. An order stopped before production was received.

Moving between them​

  • By hand, both ways. Draft, Entered, Placed, Production and In Transit are yours. Step forward as the order progresses, or step back if you moved too far. You cannot hand move an order down out of Partial or Received, because those belong to receipts.
  • By receipts. Each delivery of finished product is a receipt with its own date and lines, allowed once the order is in Production. Recording one moves the status for you, to Partial if it does not complete the run, or to Received if it does. Delete a receipt and the status walks back on its own, to Partial, or back to where the order was before anything was produced once none remain.
  • Completing. A received order can be marked Completed, and a partial order can be closed short to Completed. Either can be reverted.
  • Cancelling. Cancel stops an order before production is received. You cannot cancel one that already has receipts, so delete those first. A cancelled order can be reactivated, and it returns to wherever it last was.

Effect on inventory​

What happensLedger effect
Enter the orderbooks the output as on order and demands the materials
Move into Productionallocates the materials
Record a receiptconsumes the materials and produces the finished output
Cancelreverses the demand-side bookings back to zero

The receipt is where costing happens: consumed materials are valued at their weighted-average cost, and that per-unit material cost is added to the order's production cost to value each finished unit. Every entry carries the production-order number as its reference.

One rule keeps the ledger tidy: anything you undo is erased, not stamped with a counter entry. Step an order back out of Production and the allocation is simply removed. Delete a receipt and its consume and produce movements are removed. The only lasting reversal is a cancellation, and even that disappears if you reactivate the order. The ledger always reflects only what is true right now.

Dates​

Every order carries dates, designed to stay truthful with the least fuss.

  • Order date is yours. It defaults to today when you create the order and you can change it whenever.
  • Status dates (entered, production, and so on) stamp themselves as the order reaches each status, but stay editable afterwards so you can correct them.
  • Partial and Received dates come straight from the receipts: the first receipt's date and the completing receipt's date. They always match reality, so change a receipt's date and they follow.

When a date changes, the matching inventory movement re-dates with it, so the stock history always reads in true business date order.

Work orders vs production orders​

Work orderProduction order
You are paying forConversion / labourThe whole manufactured product
InputsMaterials and component products (BOM one level deep)Raw materials only (BOM fully exploded)
Output costConversion cost onlyProduction cost plus consumed material cost
Finished-goods valuationLabour portionFully landed cost
Reach for it whenYou own the materials and just need them assembledYou want a turnkey product costed end to end

Both share the same lifecycle and state machine. The distinction is what counts as an input and how the finished good is valued.

Open questions​

A few things we have deliberately parked, written down so they are not lost.

  1. What really separates Placed from In Transit? Draft, Entered and Production each change the ledger (nothing, then on order plus demand, then allocate), but Placed and In Transit do not. They stay cosmetic steps until they earn a meaning.
  2. Closing short needs its own rules. An order can be closed from Partial to Completed even though only part of the output was produced. What should happen to the unproduced remainder still on order, and to the materials demanded but never consumed, is an open decision.
  3. Over-receipt is not capped. Producing more than ordered is allowed and still reads Received. Left open for now.
  4. Do we need both planning dates? An order carries an order date and an expected delivery date, both free to set by hand. It is not yet clear both earn their place.
