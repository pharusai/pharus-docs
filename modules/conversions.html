Conversions

A conversion repacks finished goods at one location: cases broken back into eaches, product moved from the wrong pouch into the right one. It consumes one input product, receives one output product, and consumes the replacement packaging the output needs. It exists to undo a mistake, not to produce planned output, and its packaging never comes back to stock: this is the sanctioned way to move stock between packaging levels now that stock is consumed at the level it was counted in.

What it does​

  • One input, one output, on the spot. A conversion holds one location, one input line (a product and a quantity), and one output line: a product from the input's family, meaning the same product, its base product, or a case that packs the same base. The output's quantity is never typed; it's derived from the input quantity through the case factors that relate the two products, and an eaches-to-cases conversion must divide evenly or it's rejected. It has no reason field, and it checks nothing against stock on hand: it just records what happened. A conversion opens under a server-assigned code (CNV-001, CNV-002, and so on) that is not editable, similar to transfer codes.
  • Fill in the packaging for you. Repackaging a product almost always uses up some packaging and frees some other packaging. A conversion works this out from the input's and output's bills of materials, deriving the consumed materials the output needs beyond what the input already carried. You can add extra units on top of that, but only of materials that belong to the output's bill of materials, a fresh pouch for a repouch being the standard case.

How it works​

A conversion carries no state of its own to move through. It is the record of something that already happened, not a draft on its way to becoming one: nothing about a repack is sequential the way shipping and receiving are, so there's no stage to sit in and none to advance out of. Its ledger rows are written the moment it's created, and every field, its location, its lines, its notes, its date, stays editable afterward, with the rows kept in step.

The date​

A conversion carries one conversion date, defaulting to today, which is the business date of every row it writes. It's editable and can be back-dated. Changing it re-dates all of the conversion's ledger rows in place, so a conversion always points at a single consistent moment even after the fact.

Effect on inventory​

Line roleLedger effect
InputFinished-goods CONSUME at the conversion's location
OutputFinished-goods RECEIVE at the conversion's location
Consumed materialMaterials CONSUME at the conversion's location

These rows are written as soon as the conversion is created, on the conversion date, and carry the conversion's code as their reference, so its entries are easy to find together. A conversion writes no DEMAND, ALLOCATE, or ORDER: it's never a commitment the rest of the system should plan around, only a fact once it exists. Nothing checks stock on hand before consuming it: the ledger goes negative if it must, exactly as a transfer or a disposal does today.

Editing a line does not reverse and rewrite: the existing ledger row is updated in place, its product, quantity, location, conversion date, and notes, rather than backed out and replaced. Adding an extra material adds a row, removing one removes its row. Deleting the conversion removes all of its rows outright, undoing it completely: undo is delete. The ledger keeps no edit trail for a conversion; its cost is derived on read and recomputes from whatever rows exist now.

Consumed materials and waste​

A conversion's line table holds three roles: the input line (the one product consumed), the output line (the one product received), and the consumed lines, materials used up as replacement packaging, each either derived or added as an extra.

The consumed lines start out derived: Pharus explodes the input's and the output's products down through their bills of materials to the raw materials underneath (a component that is itself a product recurses into its own bill of materials), and whatever the output needs beyond what the input already carried becomes a derived line, shown read-only. A case converting to its base, and a product converting to itself, both derive nothing, so a straight repouch is expressed purely through extras.

On top of the derived lines, you can add extra units of any material on the output's exploded bill of materials, a fresh pouch for a repouch being the standard case; a material outside that bill of materials is rejected. Derivation reruns whenever the input or output changes and replaces the derived lines outright. An extra survives that rerun unless the new output's bill of materials no longer carries its material, in which case the extra is dropped along with it.

Waste isn't stored: it's computed on read, from the same explosion, as whatever the input carried that the output doesn't keep, plus every extra unit added, since an extra pouch means the old one was wasted. It's shown on the document. Waste isn't reported as a figure in money, but its value isn't dropped either: it flows into the output through the rule in Costing below.

Costing​

Inputs and consumed materials are ordinary outflows: they draw down real cost layers the same way any other CONSUME does. The output's RECEIVE then costs what the conversion took off the shelf, the value its input consume drew plus the value of every material it consumed, divided by the units it produced. It never reads the output product's own pool, which at the moment a conversion fills it is usually empty and always the wrong format.

Take 5 cases valued at 27.00 each. Converting 2 of them into 24 loose eaches consumes 54.00 and receives 24 eaches at 2.25, the case's cost split over the 12 eaches it held. Packing up works the same way from the other side: 12 eaches at 2.00 plus a 1.50 box and 0.50 of tape make one case worth 26.00. Value in, value out, nothing created or destroyed by the repackaging itself.

What still goes untracked is narrow: an extra that replaces material already priced inside the input. A repouch consumes fresh pouches while the old ones are still part of what the input was worth, so the output briefly carries both. It's small, and every row sits under the conversion's own reference, so it stays recomputable (see the DESIGN: comment at the costing branch in backend/src/components/costing.ts).
