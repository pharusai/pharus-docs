Inventory Costing

Inventory costing is how Pharus puts a value on stock: the unit cost carried by an outflow when something is consumed, shipped, or disposed, and the value of what remains on hand. It is not a separate store. Cost is derived from the same materials and finished goods ledgers that already record every movement, read through whichever cost model the organization is configured to use.

How costing works​

Only movements of physical stock carry cost. Inflows seed value into a location, outflows draw it back out, and the planning events in between move nothing physical and so carry no cost at all.

RoleEventsEffect on cost
InflowRECEIVE, PRODUCESeeds a cost layer at the receiving location
OutflowCONSUME, DISPOSE, TRANSFERDraws against existing layers
PlanningORDER, DEMAND, ALLOCATE, SETTLE_ORDER, SETTLE_ALLOCATENone; no stock moves

The canonical meaning of each event lives in the ledger events overview. Costing simply cares which of them put stock into a location and which take it out.

Cost layers and draws​

Each inflow is a layer: a quantity received at a known unit cost, at one material or product and one location, stamped with the business event date (and, once receipts capture them, a lot number and expiration date). Layers accumulate as stock arrives.

An outflow draws against those layers. It takes quantity from one layer after another until its own quantity is filled, and its unit cost is the weighted cost of what it drew. A transfer draws the source location's layers and re-seeds the destination at the cost it drew, so value travels with the stock rather than resetting.

Costing is kept at the grain of one item at one location. The value of stock in one warehouse is never blended with another's, and a movement is always costed against the place it actually left.

The cost models​

The four models are the same mechanism with a different order of layers. Each one only decides which layer an outflow draws from first; the draw itself is identical.

ModelDraws layers in order ofIn one line
FIFOEvent date, oldest firstFirst in, first out: the oldest stock leaves first
LIFOEvent date, newest firstLast in, first out: the newest stock leaves first
FEFOExpiration date, soonest firstFirst expired, first out: stock closest to expiry leaves first
Weighted average(no order)Every outflow draws at the blended average of remaining stock

FIFO, LIFO, and FEFO are pure orderings over the same layers. Weighted average is the one model that ignores order: it values every outflow at the running average cost of what is on hand. FEFO only has teeth once receipts carry expiration dates; until then it has nothing to order by.

Configuring it per organization​

Costing is a setting, not a code path. Each organization carries two related attributes on its settings:

  • Operating model says what kind of company it is (a manufacturer that buys materials and makes goods, or a reseller that buys finished goods and sells them). It shapes which parts of the product are in play.
  • Costing method selects the active cost model from the list above. FIFO is the safe default.

Changing the costing method changes how stock is valued from then on. There is no migration to run and no recalculation to trigger: the next outflow is simply costed under the newly selected model.

Adding a new model​

Because every model is just an ordering rule over the same layers, adding one is small. Write the new ordering as a strategy, register it under a method name, and add that name to the costing-method options an organization can choose. Nothing else in the system needs to know which model is active. The code that records an outflow asks for the cost under the organization's method and gets it back; it never branches on FIFO versus LIFO versus anything else.

What stays fixed when you switch​

The unit cost recorded on a past outflow is history and is never rewritten. A change of costing method is forward-looking: it governs how new outflows are costed and how remaining stock is valued from that point on, not how already-recorded movements were priced. Switching a method mid-life is meant to be a rare event, not a routine toggle.
